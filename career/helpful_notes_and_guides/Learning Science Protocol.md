# Learning Science Protocol

*The "how do I actually make things stick" reference. Built 2026-08-13 from live research mapped against this system's own logs. Pull this up when a concept won't stay landed, when a session felt good but nothing survived a week, or when you want to know why `/learn`, `/sharpen`, and `/drill` are shaped the way they are.*

**The one-line version: you don't have a retention problem, you have a testing-schedule problem. What you keep is decided by how and when you're asked to produce it — not by how well it was explained to you.**

> **Scope.** Its sibling `Focus & Time Block Protocol.md` owns **attention and initiation** — getting into the block and staying there. This doc owns **encoding and retention** — what survives the block. If the problem is starting, read that one. If the problem is that it didn't stick, read this one.

---

## The Learning Protocol — run this for any acquisition rep

1. **Guess before you're taught, and guess out loud even when you're sure you're wrong.** The wrong guess is the mechanism, not an embarrassment — errors followed by correction beat error-free reading. (Science #3.)
2. **One idea, then a check.** Never take a 40-minute explanation and quiz at the end. Teach ~8 minutes → 2–4 checks → a 3–8 line snippet → stop. The feedback has to land inside the chunk that earned it. (Science #6.)
3. **Vary the form.** Fill-in-the-blank, short answer, spot-the-bug — mixed, never the same twice in a row. A mixture of question types produces a stronger effect than repeating one. (Science #1.)
4. **Write down what you got wrong, in the moment, with the correction.** That's the `Tripped` list. Do not trust yourself to remember which parts were shaky — that judgment is exactly the unreliable one. (Science #5.)
5. **Build something with it before you call it learned.** Five minutes counts. Production with no scaffolding is the top rung; everything below it is preparation for it. (Science #4.)
6. **Do not mark it `[x]` in the same session you learned it.** Banking happens later — the worksheet running green, or a cold re-ask at the start of a future session. (Science #5.)
7. **Re-ask it after a real gap, mixed in with other topics.** Days to weeks, not hours, and interleaved with unrelated material. (Science #2 and #7.)

**Worst-day minimum:** recall **one** thing, cold, out loud, without looking. Three minutes. That's a rep.

## Placement rules

- **The acquisition rep belongs in the medicated peak window.** This isn't a preference — retrieval practice helps ADHD learners as much as anyone, but it *does not rescue material that was poorly encoded in the first place*, and encoding is where unmedicated ADHD measurably suffers. Testing yourself later can't fix a bad intake. (Science #1.)
- **`/drill` belongs anywhere.** Retrieval is cheap and portable; it's the one part of this that works fine at 8:30pm on a phone.
- **Nothing gets re-read.** Rereading, highlighting, and summarizing are the three lowest-utility techniques in the literature and they're the three that feel most productive. If a session consisted of reading notes, it did not happen.
- **Spacing gap ≈ 10–20% of how long you want to keep it.** Want it in six months? Review at a few weeks. A concept reviewed cleanly two days ago is the *wrong* thing to drill today. (Science #2.)
- **Drop the scaffolding as soon as it stops helping.** Worksheets, hints, and worked examples that helped last month actively slow you down once you're past them. Tedium is the signal to skip a rung, not a discipline failure. (Science #4.)

## When something won't stick — diagnostic order

Work down; stop at the first yes.

1. **Did you produce it, or did you recognize it?** → Nodding along to an explanation isn't retrieval. Close the file and say it out loud from nothing.
2. **Was the check in the same sitting as the teaching?** → Then it proved nothing. Immediate self-assessment is the least accurate moment there is. Re-ask tomorrow.
3. **Did you learn it outside the peak window / off meds?** → The encoding was thin, and no amount of later drilling fully compensates. Re-teach it in the window rather than drilling harder.
4. **Was the material blocked** (one topic, many angles, one sitting)? → It felt like mastery and produced little transfer. Interleave it with unrelated topics.
5. **Is there code that uses it?** → If not, it isn't learned yet, it's heard. Five minutes of real use.
6. **Is it on a `Tripped` list that never gets re-asked?** → The list only works if `/drill` and Step 1.5 actually consume it. That's a process leak, not a memory failure.
7. **Still nothing after all six?** → The bar is probably wrong, not you. A `[C]` you keep trying to own at `[A]` depth will feel like permanent failure. Re-bar it.

## The science (why each rule exists)

1. **Retrieval practice is the highest-return thing available, and it works for ADHD.** Producing an answer from memory beats restudying it — **g = 0.50** vs reading ([Rowland 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25150680/)), **g = 0.61** vs all other study activities ([Adesope et al. 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1529100612453266)). It is one of only two techniques rated **high utility** by [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1529100612453266) — the other is distributed practice. **Rereading, highlighting, and summarizing all rate *low*.** Three findings shape the protocol directly: the effect is *larger* when material is complex, when retrieval is effortful, and when feedback is given; and **a mixture of question formats beats a single repeated format**. Students with ADHD [benefit from retrieval practice comparably to controls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10715433/) — **but** unmedicated students [perform worse at both encoding and final test, using fewer deep strategies at intake, and retrieval practice does not compensate for it](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10397724/). That's the mechanism behind "the peak window is sacred": you cannot drill your way out of a bad intake.
2. **Spacing, with actual numbers.** [Cepeda et al. 2006](https://augmentingcognition.com/assets/Cepeda2006.pdf) — 839 assessments across 317 experiments — found **d ≈ 0.71** for spaced over massed practice; applied classroom settings give a more conservative [**d ≈ 0.54**](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12189222/). The load-bearing detail is the *interval*: the optimal gap scales with how long you want to retain, at roughly **10–20% of the target retention interval**. Massed practice wins on an immediate test and loses on every delayed one — which is precisely the trap, because the immediate test is the one you can feel.
3. **Guessing wrong first is better than reading.** The pretesting / errorful-generation effect: attempting an answer before instruction improves later recall *even when nearly all the attempts are wrong*, and [pretesting reliably beats read-only](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12292081/). The mechanism is attention to the correction — [a guess that gets violated by feedback produces surprise, and the surprise is what drives the memory](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09658211.2019.1647247). Timing matters: the guess, the feedback, and the test need to be close together. Note the twist — **learners are typically unaware this is helping**, which is why it has to be a protocol rule rather than something you'd choose in the moment.
4. **Guidance fading — the ladder from example to project.** Cognitive Load Theory's documented progression is **worked example → completion problem (partially solved) → independent problem**, [with the scaffolding removed one step at a time](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1023/B:TRUC.0000021815.74806.f6.pdf) ([Sweller on guidance fading](https://cogscisci.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/sweller-guidance-fading.pdf)). The fade is not politeness — it's the **[expertise reversal effect](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/226748784_The_expertise_reversal_effect_and_worked_examples_in_tutored_problem_solving)**: guidance that measurably helps a novice measurably *harms* someone with prior knowledge. This is the Ladder in `/learn`, and it's the same thing Reese arrived at independently on 2026-07-28 (`career/patterns.md`, the fading-scaffold row): *"support that never fades is a prosthetic; support that fades to zero is a teacher."*
5. **Your confidence right after learning is the least reliable signal you have — and this is an ADHD-specific weak point.** Adults with ADHD can judge their own memory accurately under some conditions, and notably show **[higher relative accuracy for *delayed* judgments](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17085626/)** (Knouse, Paradise & Dunlosky 2006) — but [calibration and resolution are documented weak points, trending toward overconfidence](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12144-026-09164-9). Concretely, in this system: the 2026-08-11 session produced four misconceptions that survived confident-sounding answers, and a unit nearly got banked on an explain-back that was 80% right. Hence two rules: **the `[x]` moves out of the teaching session**, and **`Tripped` gets written down rather than remembered**.
6. **Reinforcement has to land fast — faster for you than for most people.** ADHD carries a **steeper and shorter delay-of-reinforcement gradient**: the window in which a consequence still attaches to the behavior that produced it is narrower, and [feedback timing measurably changes learning outcomes in adults with ADHD](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-33551-3). Reinforcers outside that window don't land at all. This is why checks fire *per chunk* rather than at session end, why the build is five minutes rather than a weekend, and why a stopped-at line beats an unresolved thread. It's also the missing citation under the 2026-07-27 reward-loop note (`ideas_and_connections/reflection_log/2026-07-27.md`) — that note had the design right and no evidence attached.
7. **Interleaving — worse in the room, better afterward.** Mixing topics rather than blocking them is worth about **[g ≈ 0.42](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/335004545_Similarity_matters_A_meta-analysis_of_interleaved_learning_and_its_moderators)**, and the benefit lands specifically on **retention and transfer** rather than immediate performance — [including for learners who never noticed the sequencing](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-021-00110-x). Blocked practice produces knowledge that's more fragile and more dependent on the conditions it was learned under. The honest caveat: interleaving *feels worse while you do it*, generates more errors, and reliably gets rated as the less effective method by the people it just helped.

## What this protocol deliberately does NOT ask

- **No new tools.** Everything here is already implemented in `/learn`, `/sharpen`, and `/drill`. This doc explains why those steps exist so they're easier to trust on a day when a session feels bad.
- **No spaced-repetition app.** `/drill` reading `Last Reviewed` and the `Tripped` lists is a good-enough Leitner. Anki is a tool for thousands of discrete facts; this is dozens of concepts that need defending, not recognizing.
- **No tracking of scores, streaks, or retention curves.** Measuring the learning is not the learning, and it's a well-worn route into system-tinkering.
- **No expectation that the good methods feel good.** Pretesting, interleaving, and spacing all feel worse than rereading while producing more. **Feeling fluent is not evidence.** If a session felt smooth and easy, that is mild evidence it didn't work.
